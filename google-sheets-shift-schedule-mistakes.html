Seven mistakes in a Google Sheets shift schedule, and the formulas that find them

Most articles about staff scheduling in Google Sheets hand you a template and then tell you to buy an app. This one does neither. It goes through the seven things that actually go wrong in a work schedule built on a grid, gives you working formulas for the four you can catch yourself, and is straight with you about the three where a formula runs out of road.

The grid these formulas assume

Every formula below refers to real cell ranges, so you need to know which ranges. Change them to match yours, but change them everywhere.

Staff

A2:A25 holds one name per row. Nothing else on this sheet matters for the checks, though a weekly hour limit per person is handy in column D.

Shifts

A2:A15 is the shift code, B the start time, C the end time. Times, not text. If 07:00 sits on the left of the cell it is text and the arithmetic below will fail.

Schedule

A2:A25 repeats the names, B1:AF1 holds the 31 dates as real dates, B2:AF25 is the grid you type codes into. One code per cell, for now.

Hours, Runs, Problems

Three extra sheets the formulas write into. Nothing is typed on them by hand. Keeping the working out off the roster is the single best decision in this whole setup.

First, teach the sheet how long a shift is

Nothing else works without this. Put it in Shifts!D2. 

=ARRAYFORMULA(IF(A2:A15="","",MOD(C2:C15-B2:B15,1)*24))

The MOD is the whole point. A night shift from 23:00 to 07:00 gives C-B = -16 hours, which is nonsense, and MOD turns it into 8. Subtracting times directly is the most common single error in a hand-built shift planner, and it under-reports every night shift you run. There is a page of its own on this: overnight shift hours in Google Sheets, covering unpaid breaks, which day a night shift belongs to, and times that are secretly text.

One thing to know: a shift whose start and end are identical comes out as 0 rather than 24. If you genuinely run 24-hour shifts, split them.

1 and 2. Names that do not line up

A roster drifts. Someone leaves, someone is hired, a name gets typed as Kate on one sheet and Katie on the other, and from then on that person has no hour limit and no pay rate applied to them.

Put these on the Problems sheet. Column B, three rows.

In the grid but not on the staff list

=IFERROR(TEXTJOIN(", ",TRUE,UNIQUE(FILTER(Schedule!A2:A25,
  Schedule!A2:A25<>"",COUNTIF(Staff!$A$2:$A$25,Schedule!A2:A25)=0))),"none")

On the staff list but never rostered

=IFERROR(TEXTJOIN(", ",TRUE,FILTER(Staff!A2:A25,
  Staff!A2:A25<>"",COUNTIF(Schedule!$A$2:$A$25,Staff!A2:A25)=0)),"none")

The same person on two rows

=IFERROR(TEXTJOIN(", ",TRUE,UNIQUE(FILTER(Schedule!A2:A25,
  Schedule!A2:A25<>"",COUNTIF(Schedule!$A$2:$A$25,Schedule!A2:A25)>1))),"none")

The second one is worth more than it looks. Every employee scheduling tool tells you who is double booked. Almost none tell you who you forgot to put on the rota at all, which is the mistake you find out about at 07:00 when nobody opens up.

All three depend on COUNTIF matching text exactly, which means a trailing space defeats them. Wrap the ranges in TRIM if your names come from a form.

3. A shift code nobody defined

You type N for night. One Tuesday you type n, or Nn, or N with a space. The cell looks fine. It is worth zero hours in every total you calculate from it.

Catch it where you can see it. Select Schedule!B2:AF25, then Format, Conditional formatting, Custom formula is: 

=AND(B2<>"",COUNTIF(Shifts!$A$2:$A$15,B2)=0)

Set it to a red fill. Every cell holding a code that is not in your shift library goes red the moment you type it. It takes a minute to set up and catches more than anything else here.

Better than catching it is preventing it. Select the same range, Data, Data validation, Dropdown from a range, and point it at Shifts!A2:A15. Now the grid only accepts codes that exist. Shift management gets a lot quieter when typing is no longer possible.

4. Hours per person, and the weekly limit

This is the one that costs money. Overtime is almost always defined per week, and a month grid tempts you into adding up the month. Fifty hours one week and thirty the next averages to forty and hides ten hours of overtime.

On the Hours sheet, put this in B2 and drag it down to row 25. It turns the whole grid into hours. 

=ARRAYFORMULA(IF(Schedule!B2:AF2="",0,
  IFERROR(VLOOKUP(Schedule!B2:AF2,Shifts!$A$2:$D$15,4,FALSE),0)))

One formula per row, 31 columns wide. The month total for a person is then just =SUM(B2:AF2), and you have a second use for it: a cell with something typed in it that produced 0 hours is a typo. On the Problems sheet: 

=SUMPRODUCT((Schedule!$B$2:$AF$25<>"")*(Hours!$B$2:$AF$25=0))

Now the weekly part. Put your week start dates in Hours!AH1 going right, one Monday per column, then this in AH2 and drag it right and down. 

=SUMPRODUCT((Schedule!$B$1:$AF$1>=AH$1)*(Schedule!$B$1:$AF$1<AH$1+7)*$B2:$AF2)

That is hours per person per week, counted by actual date rather than by column position. Put your limit in a spare cell, say AO1 = 48, and conditional format AH2:AM25 with =AH2>$AO$1.

Here is the part the template articles leave out. The first and last weeks of any month are cut in half. Someone who worked 20 hours on the 29th, 30th and 31st of last month and 32 hours on the 1st and 2nd of this one is over 48 for that week, and no formula living on this month's sheet can see it. A monthly grid and a weekly limit disagree about where the week starts, and that disagreement is structural, not a bug you can formula your way out of. There is a page of its own on that week: weekly overtime on a monthly rota, with the three ways round it and what each one costs.

5. Too many days in a row

Eleven days without a day off does not look like anything on a grid. Each cell is innocent. It is the run that is the problem.

This one needs a helper sheet, and the reason is worth understanding. Counting a run means each day's answer depends on yesterday's answer, and an array formula cannot refer to its own output. So on the Runs sheet, B2 is: 

=IF(Schedule!B2="",0,1)

and C2 is:

=IF(Schedule!C2="",0,B2+1)

Drag C2 right to AF2, then select B2:AF2 and drag the whole thing down to row 25. The longest run for a person is =MAX(B2:AF2). Flag anything over your limit with =IF(MAX(B2:AF2)>6,"check","").

There is a single-cell version of this using MMULT. It works and it is unreadable, and the next person to inherit your spreadsheet will not be able to change it. A visible helper row is the better engineering decision even though it looks less clever.

Same month boundary problem as the weekly limit: a run that started on the 28th of last month reads as starting on the 1st.

Where formulas stop

Two of the seven I would not attempt in a spreadsheet, and one I would attempt only if the grid stays simple. Being clear about which is which saves you an afternoon.

6. Rest between two shifts

This is the check that matters most for the people on the rota and it is the one a spreadsheet handles worst. To know the gap you need the exact moment the previous shift ended, as a date and a time together, and for a night shift that moment is on the following calendar day. So you need a second mirror grid for start times: 

=ARRAYFORMULA(IF(Schedule!B2:AF2="","",
  IFERROR(VLOOKUP(Schedule!B2:AF2,Shifts!$A$2:$D$15,2,FALSE),"")))

a third for end moments, built from the date, the start and the length:

=IF(Schedule!B2="","",Schedule!B$1+Starts!B2+Hours!B2/24)

and a fourth for the gap itself, in hours:

=IF(OR(Schedule!C2="",Schedule!B2=""),"",(Schedule!C$1+Starts!C2-Ends!B2)*24)

That works. It is also four sheets of scaffolding to check one rule, and it still only compares a day against the day immediately before it. A gap that spans a day off is not measured, which is usually harmless and occasionally not, when the earlier shift ran past midnight. Put two codes in one cell and the whole thing returns the wrong shift's times without telling you.

7. Two shifts in one cell that overlap each other

Writing M/A in a cell for a double is a reasonable habit. Checking whether those two shifts collide is not reasonable to do with formulas. SPLIT gives you a variable number of parts, so a formula written for two codes silently ignores the third, and you have to compare every pair of start and end times against every other pair. You can at least count them. On the Problems sheet: 

=COUNTIF(Schedule!B2:AF25,"*/*")

That tells you how many cells hold more than one shift. Whether they overlap is a different question and that is where I would stop.

And one thing no formula can fix

A schedule is a plan. Attendance is what happened. Keep them on separate sheets, because the moment you start editing last week's rota to record who actually turned up, you have destroyed the only record of what you asked people to do. Copy the month, rename the copy, and let the plan stand. The timesheets you hand to payroll come off the attendance copy. The plan is what you promised, and it is worth being able to look back at.
